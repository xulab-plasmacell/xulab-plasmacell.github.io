Game (Conway’s Game of Life)

A niche game about the conditions that keep a cell alive.

Maintained by the Xu Lab — Prof. Chunliang Xu, Sun Yat-sen University.

Why a niche game belongs on this site

The Game of Life is a niche game in the precise sense: no score, no opponent, no ending. Its audience is small, its rules fit on one line, and its rewards are observational — you watch a rule system unfold and learn to recognize the structures it permits. It is less a game of play than a game of conditions. The same five-cell seed can vanish, oscillate or travel forever, and which one happens depends entirely on the local configuration of neighbors around each cell.

Plasma cells obey the same logic. A long-lived plasma cell is not immortal in itself; it persists because it occupies a niche — a specific address in the bone marrow where stromal cells, chemokines and survival factors hold it alive. Take the same cell out of that address and it is gone within days. In the Game of Life a cell’s fate is decided the same way, by how many neighbors surround it: two or three and it survives, zero or one and it dies of isolation, four or more and it dies of crowding. Conway’s B3/S23 is, literally, a niche definition — a narrow band of local conditions that permits persistence and nothing more.

That is why the page is here. Every preset in the menu is a different niche: the same starting pattern, placed under different survival conditions, will thrive, freeze or collapse. Switching presets is the cheapest experiment we know for the question our laboratory actually asks — how much of a cell’s lifespan is written into the cell, and how much is written into where it lives.

How the rules read · S (survive): the number of neighbors a living cell needs in order to stay alive. B (born): the number a dead cell needs in order to come to life.

How to use this page

Nothing to win here: the canvas is a grid of cells, and every step recomputes each cell from its eight neighbours and nothing else. What you control is the rule that decides the outcome, and the starting pattern the rule acts on.

Playback and the canvas

Start / Pause
Runs the simulation, one generation every Speed milliseconds. The label flips to Pause while it runs.
Step
Pauses, then advances exactly one generation — the way to watch a pattern move frame by frame.
Canvas click
Toggles a single cell. The generation counter keeps going, but a later Jump rebuilds the pattern and discards the edit.
Random
Fills the grid at 5%, 15% or 30%. Whatever it produces becomes generation 0.
Clear
Empties the grid and zeroes the generation and cell count — the cleanest way to draw by hand.
Speed
Delay between generations, 5–500 ms. Lower is faster; dragging it mid-run restarts the loop but not the counter.
Cell size
The canvas is fixed at 800 × 600 px, so 10 px cells give 80 × 60 and 20 px cells give 40 × 30. Resizing keeps the top-left content and resets to generation 0.

Rules

Survive (S)
Neighbour counts at which a living cell stays alive; tick 2 and 3 for the classic rule.
Born (B)
Neighbour counts at which a dead cell comes to life; tick only 3 for the classic rule.
Apply
Writes all eighteen checkboxes into the rule actually in force. Ticked boxes do nothing until you press it — press Start without Apply and the previous rule still runs.
Presets
Applies a named rule the moment you select it, no Apply needed, and moves the checkboxes to match so you can see what it uses.

Patterns, files and jumping

Place
Drops the selected pattern at the canvas centre, erasing whatever it covers — no drag and drop in this build. The result is generation 0.
Save settings
Downloads a JSON file holding the grid size, speed, rule and every cell. Nothing is stored on a server; the file is the only copy.
Load settings
Reads such a file back and rejects it with a message if a required field is missing or the grid size does not match.
Jump
Recomputes generation N from the stored starting pattern without animating the steps. There is no upper limit, so a very large N will freeze the page for a moment.

Nothing you change redraws the canvas on its own — a new rule only shows up in the next generation. The order that works: tick → Apply → Step.

Rules and presets

Six rules are provided, each written as Bcounts/Scounts — the neighbor counts that create a cell, followed by the counts that keep one alive. Changing the rule is the whole experiment: the same starting pattern will thrive, freeze or vanish depending on which numbers appear here.

Conway’s Game of Life
B3/S23
The original rule from 1970. A living cell survives with two or three neighbors and dies of isolation or crowding otherwise; a dead cell is born with exactly three. Balanced enough to sustain motion: gliders travel, blinkers oscillate, guns emit. Start here — every pattern in the menu is meant to be seen under this rule first..
Low threshold
B2/S3
Birth needs only two neighbors instead of three, so growth starts far more easily: quick expansion, then a stall into large empty regions separated by thin walls. To see how a lower birth threshold changes the character of growth..
Life without Death
B3/S012345678
Every living cell survives at any neighbor count from 0 to 8, so nothing ever dies. Patterns crystallize permanently and grow outward in a fixed texture. To demonstrate growth without decay — “survival” is a rule, not a property of the cell..
HighLife
B36/S23
Conway’s rule plus birth at six neighbors. The extra condition admits a replicator, a small figure that copies itself indefinitely. To show that one extra number in the rule can create an entirely new class of behavior..
Dense
B35/S23
Birth at five neighbors as well as three. Patterns grow thicker and blockier, with compact masses replacing the delicate structures of B3/S23. To compare a permissive but not explosive rule against the classic one..
Fast spread
B2/S23
Birth at two neighbors while survival still requires two or three. Growth is faster than Conway’s and tends to form bands and streaks rather than isolated figures. Compare it directly with B2/S3 above to see what the survival rule contributes..
